# German caption voice — what the sources actually say

_Session 180, 2026-08-17. Commissioned to validate or correct the German section of
`functions/caption/caption-voice.md`, written earlier the same session. The owner challenged one
rule specifically: "do not start with Ein/Eine — is it really slop in German?"_

**Verdict up front: he was right to challenge it. The rule is wrong as written, and two of the
sources say something close to the opposite.** Three other rules in the guide are confirmed by
authoritative German sources. One new German-specific rule should be added.

Scope note: this is a focused note, not the full deep-research report template. The question is
narrow and directly actionable, and a long report would bury the answer. Sources are listed with a
confidence marker, because several claims come from search-result extracts rather than a direct
read of the page — that distinction is recorded rather than smoothed over.

## 0. Correction, added after owner review

The owner read this note and rejected the sourcing, correctly: **sources [3] and [4] are
professional journalism guidance and must not be the basis for a decision about lifestyle photobook
captions.** §2 below says exactly that, and then §1 leaned on them anyway. Two consequences:

1. **The removal of the Ein/Eine rule does not depend on them, and never did.** It rests on the
   absence of any German authority treating a sentence-initial article as a defect, plus the fact
   that the rule was invented by analogy with English. The burden of proof sat on the rule.
   [3] and [4] are demoted to context; they are not evidence for how Aevia writes.
2. **The replacement rule about fragments was re-grounded on in-genre evidence.** Aevia's own
   authored German — Xenia's, the only lifestyle German we hold — has exactly two fragments:
   *"Unser süßer kleiner Bub."* and *"Der Anfang von für immer."* Both are led by a determiner;
   neither is a bare noun. That is better evidence than any journalism handbook, it is in the right
   genre, and it is in the right brand voice. The guide now cites it instead.

Also worth recording, because it cuts against our own rules: Xenia's Newborn labour page reads
*"Willkommen auf der Welt, Nico!"* — **with an exclamation mark**, which both the English and German
voice sections forbid. That is book text on a fixed page rather than a caption, so the two are not
in direct conflict, but it is a reminder that her German is warmer than the caption rules allow and
that the caption rules are ours, not hers.

What no source anywhere supports either way: whether an Aevia German caption *should* open with
"Ein/Eine". There is no in-brand example. If the owner wants that kept as a house preference it is a
legitimate brand-voice choice — it just cannot be presented as a German style rule.

## 1. Is there a German rule against opening with "Ein/Eine"?

**No. Not for captions, and not in the sense the guide claimed.** I found no German style
authority that treats a sentence-initial indefinite article as a defect.

Two findings point the other way:

**German caption convention explicitly calls for articles.** The rule, stated in the same form by
two independent sources, is that captions differ from headlines precisely here: *"Im Gegensatz zu
Schlagzeilen sollten Bildunterschriften alle Artikel und auch Konjunktionen enthalten"* [4], and
captions should be *"in verständlichen, ganzen Sätzen formuliert – gerne auch mit Verben"* [4],
while *"keinesfalls wie Telegramme oder Maschinengewehre"* sounding [4]. `Das neue Handbuch des
Journalismus` is cited to the same effect [3].

**Where a real "ein/eine" rule exists, it is about something else entirely.** The
PraetoriusCC style navigator does advise avoiding "ein/eine" — but for *abstract nominalisations*
in precise German: "eine Optimierung" → "Optimierung", "eine Stärkung" → "Stärkung". The test it
offers is whether the article carries any meaning at all; if not, drop it [11]. This is a rule
about redundant articles before abstract nouns, not about how a sentence opens, and it belongs to
the same family as the Nominalstil critique below.

So the English rule did not transfer. It exists in English because "A quiet moment between
them" is a limp, stock opening in English caption writing. The German equivalent of that failure is
not the article — it is the abstract noun the article is attached to.

**Correction for the guide:** drop the prohibition on opening with Ein/Eine. Replace it with the
rule that is genuinely German: cut an indefinite article that sits in front of an abstract noun and
adds nothing, and prefer the concrete noun that needs no article at all.

## 2. A caveat that matters more than the rule itself

The caption sources above describe **journalistic** captions, whose job is to inform: say who is in
the picture, in the present tense, in full sentences, claiming nothing the photo does not show
[3][5][6]. Aevia's captions are a different genre. The English voice guide is explicit —
*"Write what the photo feels like. Not what is literally in it"* — and its examples are deliberate
fragments ("Two hours from anywhere").

**So German journalism convention must not be imported wholesale**, or Aevia's German captions
would become descriptive picture labels. Two things from it are worth taking:

1. **The one rule both genres share**: claim nothing the photo does not show, and never interpret
   what a person in the photo is thinking or feeling [3][6]. This already matches the English
   guide's "Never assume facts you cannot see."
2. **The Telegrammstil warning, which is a genuinely German risk.** German normally requires the
   article where English can drop it, so an article-less German fragment reads clipped and
   telegraphic much faster than its English counterpart does. This is an argument for *keeping*
   articles in German captions — the reverse of the rule I wrote. A fragment works in German when
   it is idiomatic ("Kein Netz, den ganzen Tag"), not merely short.

## 3. Confirmed: Nominalstil is the right primary target

Wolf Schneider's `Deutsch für Profis` (1984), the standard German style reference for professional
writers, is built on exactly this: **active verbs instead of nominal constructions, short sentences
instead of nested ones, concrete terms instead of abstractions** [1][2]. The guide's placement of
Nominalstil as "the biggest one" is correct and well founded.

## 4. Confirmed and extendable: the LLM-German markers

German-language work on detecting machine-written text converges on markers close to the guide's,
and adds concrete phrases worth naming outright [7][8][9]:

- **Opening formulas**: "In der heutigen Zeit ist es wichtiger denn je…", "In einer Welt, in
  der…", "Immer mehr Menschen fragen sich…", "Es ist wichtig zu beachten, dass…", "Viele Experten
  sind sich einig, dass…" [7]
- **The single most-cited structure**: "nicht nur…, sondern auch…" [7]
- **Overused vocabulary**: "präzise", "strukturell", "sauber", "eintauchen", "umfassend",
  "ganzheitlich" [7]
- **Three-item adjective lists** ("sicher, garantiert und zweifellos") and rule-of-three structure
  generally [7][8]
- **Gedankenstrich overuse** as artificial pauses [7]
- **Uniform sentence structure and length** — the mechanical rhythm the guide already names [8][9]
- **Generic instead of concrete**: researched text carries specifics, LLM text carries general
  formulations [9]

On advertising cliché, German copywriting sources single out **unbelegte wertende Adjektive** —
"einzigartig", "innovativ", "wirkungsvoll" used with no evidence behind them [10][13][14] — which
supports the guide's greeting-card list. The stated reason is worth carrying into the guide because
it is the useful test: *Floskeln lassen keine Bilder im Kopf entstehen* [10]. A phrase that
produces no picture is the thing to cut.

Note what is **not** corroborated by any source I found: "einfangen" as a specific German tell. It
is a well-known cliché in German photo copy and I would keep it on the list, but it is my
judgement, not a cited finding. Recorded as such.

## 6. Limitations

- **Nothing found on the actual genre.** Poetic or editorial photobook captions in German have no
  authoritative style literature that I could locate. Every caption source is journalistic. The
  gap is real and the guide's examples remain a judgement call resting on Xenia's authored German.
- **Two claims rest on search extracts, not a direct page read.** [3] could not be fetched (TLS
  certificate mismatch on journalismus-handbuch.de) and [11] was read through a search summary.
  Both are corroborated by at least one directly-read source, which is why they are used at all.
- **No native speaker has reviewed any of this**, which remains the largest unverified surface in
  the whole germanization effort.
- **Austrian usage was not researched separately.** All sources are German-market. Xenia's "Bub"
  is the only Austrian marker we hold, and it came from her, not from a source.
